What "finding gaps" actually means in Congress, and why champions win by doing the fundamentals best, not by chasing the most obscure argument.
Lesson 1 of 5
What "Finding Gaps" Actually Means

Most debaters hear "find gaps" and assume it means come up with a weird, unique argument that no one else has thought of. That's the wrong instinct, and it's how a lot of competitive debaters end up running tangential, hard-to-judge arguments while losing to speakers who are simply doing the basics better.

Here's the real definition: a gap is an unrefuted weak point in the strongest arguments others are running, or a missing piece (warrant, evidence, impact) that other speakers left out.

If three aff speakers all argue "this bill creates jobs" but none of them prove why the bill's specific mechanism actually creates those jobs, that's a gap. The argument is on the table, but the warrant is missing. You can step in and fill it.

If every neg speaker attacks cost but no one explains the human consequence of those costs, that's a gap. The impact is missing. Fill it.

Finding gaps is mostly about listening carefully and noticing what isn't being said well, not inventing brand-new arguments from scratch.

The Gap Mindset
Before your speech, ask: "What argument is everyone making, and what part of it is no one proving well?" The answer is your gap. Your job is to take the most important argument in the round and execute it better than anyone else has.
Multiple Choice
Three aff speakers have argued that a climate bill will create green jobs, but none of them have explained the specific mechanism by which the bill creates those jobs, or quantified the impact. What's the strongest gap-filling move?
Lesson 2 of 5
Strengthen the Fundamentals First

The biggest gap in most rounds isn't a missing argument, it's a poorly executed one. Speakers gesture at warrants without proving them. They cite evidence without explaining why it matters. Their impacts are abstract, not human.

This is your real opening. Not the niche angle no one thought of, but the central argument everyone is running, executed at a level no one else has reached.

Three places where most speakers leave gaps in their fundamentals:

1. Warrants. Most speakers state a claim and assume the logic is obvious. It usually isn't. If your warrant clearly walks judges through why the claim is true, step by step, you've already separated yourself from the field.

2. Evidence. A lot of speakers cite "studies say" or vaguely reference experts. Specific evidence with named sources, dates, and numbers is rare, and it dramatically increases credibility. The same argument with hard data beats the same argument with hand-waving.

3. Impacts. Most impacts stop at "this is good for the economy" or "this helps people." Champions push to the human consequence, terminalize, and connect to lives, families, or fundamental rights. (You learned this in Module 2.5, the same principle applies here.)

If you do all three of these better than the previous speakers, you've filled the most important gap of all: the gap between an argument that's been said and an argument that's been proven.

Lesson 3 of 5
When Unique Arguments DO Work

This isn't a "never run a unique argument" rule. Unique arguments can absolutely win rounds, but only when they tie back to the core of the debate.

A unique aff angle that explains why the bill is a great idea in a way no one has framed it yet? Powerful. A unique neg angle that surfaces a real harm everyone missed? Powerful.

But a unique argument that's tangential, technically related to the topic but not actually responsive to what the debate is about, will lose to a well-executed mainstream argument every time.

Two tests for whether a unique argument is worth running:

1. Does it answer the central question of the debate? If the round is about whether the bill should pass on the merits of its mechanism, a constitutional-tangent argument doesn't answer that question, it changes the subject.

2. Can the average judge follow it without context? If your unique argument requires three minutes of background just to understand, you've spent your speech building a foundation instead of making your case.

The best unique arguments feel obvious in retrospect. Judges hear them and think "Why didn't anyone else see this?" That's different from a niche argument that judges hear and think "Wait, what does this have to do with the bill?"

Unique that works
A neg speaker on a federal minimum wage bill runs the angle that small rural economies will be hurt disproportionately because their cost of living is so much lower than urban areas. Everyone else has been arguing about national-level GDP. This is unique AND directly responsive to the bill's actual mechanism.
Unique that doesn't
That same neg speaker runs an argument that the federal government shouldn't be involved in wage policy at all because it violates the original intent of the Commerce Clause. Technically related to the topic. Not responsive to the actual debate the chamber is having. Loses to almost any well-executed economic argument.

Lesson 4 of 5
How Champions Actually Win

Look at NSDA national champions. Look at TOC winners. Listen to their speeches.

You won't hear the most niche, never-before-considered arguments. You'll hear the most important argument in the round, proven the best.

Champions don't win because they outsmart the room with an obscure angle. They win because they take the central debate and execute it at a level the rest of the field can't match. Their warrants are clearer. Their evidence is more specific. Their impacts are more human. Their delivery is sharper. Their CX is more incisive.

This is what gap-filling actually looks like at the highest level. The "gap" isn't a missing argument, it's the gap between average execution and championship execution on the most important argument in the round.

The Champion's Mindset
"NSDA and TOC champions aren't made from coming up with the most niche or obscure argument. They're made by proving the most important argument the best in the round." If you remember nothing else from this module, remember that.

The practical implication: spend most of your prep time getting better at the fundamentals, not hunting for unique angles. Better warrants, better evidence, sharper impacts, cleaner delivery, sharper CX. That's the work.
